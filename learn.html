What a data center is

A data center is a building whose only job is to keep computers running: fed with reliable power, kept cool, and connected to the internet. Everything in the building exists to serve the rows of racks in the data halls.

The three flows every data center manages: power in, heat out, data in and out.

Sizes vary enormously. A small enterprise room might hold a few dozen racks and draw a few hundred kilowatts. A hyperscale campus built for AI training can cover hundreds of acres, hold tens of thousands of racks, and draw more than a gigawatt, as much as a large city.

EnterpriseOne company's own servers, often inside an office building. Tens to hundreds of racks.
ColocationA landlord rents space, power and cooling to many tenants who bring their own gear. Equinix and Digital Realty are the big names.
HyperscaleBuilt and run by one cloud or AI company for its own workloads. Amazon, Microsoft, Google, Meta, Oracle, OpenAI's Stargate partners.

The power chain

Electricity arrives from the grid at high voltage and gets stepped down several times before it reaches a server. Along the way it passes through equipment whose only purpose is to make sure the power never stops.

Dashed arrows are the backup paths. If the grid drops, batteries carry the load for the seconds it takes generators to start.
  • Substation and transformers. Large sites get their own substation on site. Waiting for the utility to build one is now the single biggest schedule risk for new projects, often three to seven years in busy markets.
  • Switchgear. Big breakers that let operators isolate equipment and automatically switch between grid and generator.
  • UPS (uninterruptible power supply). Sits between the grid and the servers, smoothing out dips and covering the gap until generators take over. Batteries are usually lithium-ion now, sized for a few minutes.
  • Generators. Diesel is standard. Enough fuel is stored on site for a day or more. Some new AI sites run gas turbines as their main supply because the grid cannot deliver in time.
  • PDUs. Power distribution units take 480 V and split it into circuits for each row, then each rack has its own strip. Critical racks get two independent feeds, A and B.

Why the grid is the story of the decade: a conventional rack draws 5 to 10 kW, an AI training rack full of GPUs draws 40 to 130 kW, and whole campuses are being planned at 1 to 5 GW. Utilities have to build new generation and transmission to keep up, which is why data center headlines now sit next to nuclear restarts, gas turbine shortages and fights over who pays for the upgrades.

Cooling

Almost every watt of electricity a server uses turns into heat, so a 100 MW data center is also a 100 MW heater. The classic approach is air: keep cold air and hot air strictly separated and move a lot of it.

Hot aisle / cold aisle containment. Racks face each other across a cold aisle; their backs exhaust into a hot aisle that is walled off so the two never mix.
  • CRAH / CRAC units. Big fan-coil cabinets at the edge of the hall. CRAH units use chilled water from the plant; CRAC units have their own refrigerant compressors.
  • Chillers and cooling towers. Outside, the heat has to go somewhere. Chillers cool the water loop; cooling towers reject heat to the air by evaporation, which is where most water use comes from. Dry coolers and "free cooling" in cold climates avoid evaporation.
  • Containment. Doors and roof panels over an aisle. Cheap, and one of the biggest efficiency wins.

AI racks changed the math. Air cannot carry away 100 kW from a single rack, so GPU halls use liquid cooling: water or coolant piped straight to cold plates sitting on the chips.

Direct-to-chip liquid cooling. The facility water loop never touches the servers; a CDU separates it from the clean loop that runs to the chips.

Inside a rack

The rack is the unit everything is measured in. A standard rack is 19 inches wide and 42 rack units (U) tall; a 1U server is 1.75 inches high. What goes in the slots decides how much power and cooling the row needs.

A mixed rack. A real AI training rack is often nothing but GPU servers plus the switches and liquid cooling manifolds that feed them.
  • Servers. Industry-standard boxes with CPUs, memory and NVMe drives. Hyperscalers design their own.
  • GPU servers. The reason for the current boom. One box of eight accelerators can draw more than an entire old rack. Racks of them are wired together into a single machine for training.
  • Storage. Shelves of drives and flash, usually in their own racks.
  • Switches. Top-of-rack switches connect the servers up to the hall's spine network.
  • Rack PDUs. Two vertical power strips, each on a different upstream path, so a failure on one side does not take the rack down.

The network

A data center is only useful if data can get in and out. Fiber from several carriers enters the building, ideally through different physical routes so one backhoe cannot cut everything. Inside, the network fans out in layers so that any server can reach any other with the same short hop count.

Spine-leaf. Every rack switch (leaf) links to every spine switch, so traffic between any two racks always crosses exactly one spine.
  • Meet-me room. The neutral space where outside carriers terminate their fiber and tenants cross-connect to them.
  • Spine-leaf fabric. Replaced the old three-tier design because modern traffic is mostly server to server (east-west), not server to internet.
  • AI back-end networks. GPU clusters add a second, separate network (InfiniBand or high-speed Ethernet) just for the GPUs to talk to each other during training. It can cost as much as the switches for the rest of the building.
  • Latency. Light in fiber covers about 200 km per millisecond. That is why trading firms cluster near exchanges, why "availability zones" are tens of kilometers apart, and why AI training clusters are built as one giant site rather than spread out.

Redundancy and tiers

"N" is the amount of equipment needed to carry the load. Redundancy means having more than N, so maintenance or a failure does not cause an outage. The shorthand turns up in every design document and sales sheet.

The same idea applies to generators, chillers, pumps and network links. 2N costs roughly twice as much as N.
Tier I and IISingle power and cooling path. Planned maintenance means downtime. Small enterprise rooms.
Tier IIIConcurrently maintainable: any component can be serviced without shutting down. The common target for colocation, around 99.98% expected availability.
Tier IVFault tolerant: any single unplanned failure is absorbed. Fully 2N. Banks, critical government, some healthcare.

The tier system is published by the Uptime Institute. Hyperscalers mostly do not bother certifying; they get resilience by running the same workload in several buildings or regions and letting software route around a failed site.

The numbers: MW, PUE, WUE

Headlines describe data centers in megawatts, not square feet, because power is the real constraint. "A 500 MW campus" means 500 MW of IT load the site can deliver to racks, not the building's total draw.

Power usage effectiveness. The gap between the blue bar and the total is overhead: cooling, transformer and UPS losses, lighting.
MW (IT load)The capacity sold or planned. 1 GW = 1,000 MW. For scale, a typical US nuclear reactor produces about 1 GW.
PUETotal power ÷ IT power. Lower is better. Driven by climate, cooling design and how hard the site is loaded.
WUELitres of water consumed per kilowatt-hour of IT energy. Evaporative cooling pushes it up; dry or liquid cooling pulls it down.
Rack densitykW per rack. 5 to 10 for ordinary cloud, 40 to 130 for AI training.
CostRoughly $10 to $15 million per MW for the building and infrastructure, before any servers. GPUs can double or triple that.
CarbonMostly from the electricity used, so it depends on the grid mix and on power purchase agreements with wind, solar and nuclear.

Why they get built where they do

Developers run the same checklist on every candidate site. Power now dominates everything else, which is why the map is filling in across rural Texas, Louisiana, Ohio, Indiana, Wisconsin and Wyoming rather than only Northern Virginia and Silicon Valley.

Power, and whenIs there spare capacity on the grid, and can the utility deliver hundreds of megawatts in two years rather than seven? Sites next to power plants, retired coal plants or gas pipelines get a head start.
LandHundreds of flat acres, zoned industrial, away from flood plains and airports, with room to expand.
FiberLong-haul fiber routes nearby, ideally from more than one carrier on different paths.
Water and climateCooler, drier climates cut cooling cost. Water rights matter where evaporative cooling is used.
Tax incentivesMost US states exempt data center equipment from sales tax. The deals are large and increasingly contested in state legislatures.
Community and permittingZoning approval, noise, truck traffic, and local opposition can add a year or kill a project. This is a growing share of the headlines.

Established hubs still matter for connectivity and low latency: Northern Virginia (the largest market in the world), Dallas, Phoenix, Chicago, Atlanta, Columbus, Portland, Silicon Valley; abroad, Frankfurt, London, Amsterdam, Paris, Dublin, Singapore, Tokyo, Sydney and Mumbai.

From announcement to switch-on

The news feed on this site tracks projects through these stages. The gap between "announced" and "operational" is typically two to four years, and the power agreement is usually the long pole.

Blue dots are where the money is spent. Campuses are built in phases, so one site can stay "under construction" for a decade.
  • Announced. A company, a county and a dollar figure. Watch for code names (Stargate, Hyperion, Fairwater) and for shell companies buying land before the real name appears.
  • Under construction. Groundbreaking, earthworks, then structural steel; a data hall shell can go up in a year, with the electrical and mechanical fit-out taking longer than the building itself.
  • Commissioning. Weeks of pulling the plug on purpose: generators, UPS and chillers are tested at full load before any customer equipment arrives.
  • Operational. Racks move in, often in waves over years. The first customer switch-on is the milestone that matters.

Glossary

Colocation (colo)Renting space and power in someone else's data center.
HyperscalerA cloud or AI company running data centers at huge scale for its own services.
CRAH / CRACComputer room air handler / air conditioner. The cooling cabinets in a data hall.
CDUCoolant distribution unit. Pumps and heat exchanger that feed liquid cooling to racks.
UPSUninterruptible power supply. Batteries and electronics that bridge grid and generator.
PDUPower distribution unit, at row level or inside the rack.
ATSAutomatic transfer switch. Flips the load from grid to generator.
PUEPower usage effectiveness. Total facility power divided by IT power.
WUEWater usage effectiveness. Water consumed per unit of IT energy.
Rack unit (U)1.75 inches of vertical rack space. Standard rack is 42U.
Meet-me roomWhere outside carriers' fiber terminates and tenants cross-connect.
Spine-leafTwo-layer network design where every rack switch connects to every spine switch.
PPAPower purchase agreement. Long-term contract to buy electricity, often from a new wind, solar or nuclear plant.
Behind the meterGeneration built on site for the data center, bypassing the public grid.
Availability zoneA cluster of data centers close enough for fast links but far enough apart to fail independently.
